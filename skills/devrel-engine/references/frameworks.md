## 1. Developer Journey Stages

Source: Lewko & Parton, "How to Build and Grow a Successful Developer Program"; adapted across DevRel literature.

The developer journey maps the ideal path from first contact to long-term platform commitment. Every content piece and community action should serve a specific stage.

**Discover → Evaluate → Learn → Build → Scale**

| Stage | User's Intent | Key Touchpoints | Content Needed |
|-------|--------------|-----------------|----------------|
| **Discover** | "What is this? Could it help me?" | Search, social, events, word of mouth, content | Showcasing: success stories, explainers, "what is X" content |
| **Evaluate** | "Is this right for my needs? How does it compare?" | Docs, comparisons, community observation, trial | Educational + Showcasing: tutorials, vs-competitor, templates, sandbox |
| **Learn** | "How do I use this effectively?" | Documentation, tutorials, getting-started guides, community Q&A | Educational: guides, walkthroughs, onboarding sequences, office hours |
| **Build** | "I'm creating something real" | Product, community, support, advanced docs | Conversational + Educational: troubleshooting, advanced tutorials, peer support |
| **Scale** | "How do I grow/maintain/professionalize what I built?" | Integrations, infrastructure, performance, enterprise features | Educational + Operational: optimization guides, integration docs, best practices |

**Critical insight:** Stage transitions are where users drop off. Evaluate→Learn and Build→Scale are the highest-risk transitions. Map your community's drop-off data to these transitions to find the biggest retention levers.

**Stages don't imply elapsed time.** A developer may complete Discover through Build in an afternoon with a frictionless experience, or take months waiting for the right use case. The journey reflects intent shifts, not calendar time.

## 2. DevRel Objectives


Four high-level objectives that map to the developer journey. Every DevRel action should advance at least one.

| Objective | Definition | Journey Stages | Examples |
|-----------|-----------|----------------|----------|
| **Awareness** | Target users know the product exists and understand its value | Discover, Evaluate | Social media, Product Hunt, conference talks, SEO, content marketing |
| **Activation** | Users try the product and have a successful first experience | Evaluate, Learn | Onboarding flows, quickstart tutorials, welcome sequences, "first success" milestones |
| **Engagement** | Active users participate in community, provide feedback, help peers | Learn, Build | Discussion prompts, events, peer support culture, feedback boards, office hours |
| **Innovation** | Users push boundaries, create novel use cases, influence roadmap | Build, Scale | Hackathons, beta programs, feature voting, co-creation, showcases |
| **Retention** | Users stay long-term rather than churning or graduating to competitors | Scale | Advanced features, integrations, champion programs, investment in user success |

Some frameworks add a fifth: **Advocacy** — users actively recruit others. This overlaps with Retention and maps to O1 Ambassadors in the Orbit Model.

### The DevRel Flywheel

Source: Synthesized from AbsolutelySkilled, industry practice.

The DevRel function itself operates as a feedback loop. Breaking any link reduces the entire function's effectiveness.

**Build** → **Educate** → **Engage** → **Listen** → back to **Build**

| Phase | Activities | Outputs |
|-------|-----------|---------|
| **Build** | SDKs, sample apps, starter kits, code templates, tools | Working artifacts developers can use immediately |
| **Educate** | Talks, tutorials, blog posts, videos, docs, workshops | Content that teaches real concepts via real problems |
| **Engage** | Community channels, support, events, office hours, hackathons | Relationships and trust with the developer community |
| **Listen** | Feedback collection, pain point cataloging, survey analysis, issue triage | Prioritized insights that flow back into Build |

The flywheel accelerates when each phase feeds the next: building something reveals what to teach, teaching reveals what confuses people, engaging reveals what they actually need, listening reveals what to build next. A DevRel team that only Educates without Listening will produce content nobody asked for. A team that only Listens without Building will collect feedback that never turns into action.

### The Compound-over-Campaign Principle

Source: securesigner/AbsolutelySkilled.

A single blog post fades; a series builds authority. A one-off talk is forgotten; a consistent conference presence builds reputation. A community event is nice; a monthly rhythm is a movement.

**Invest in content and activities that compound rather than campaigns that spike and fade.** Evergreen tutorials, maintained SDKs, active community channels, and predictable event cadences all compound. Product launch announcements, one-off hackathons, and seasonal campaigns all spike and decay.

When choosing between two activities of similar effort, pick the one with a longer half-life. When allocating time, invest the majority in compounding work and use campaigns as amplifiers on top of the compounding base — not as substitutes for it.

## 3. Orbit Model

Source: Orbit (acquired by Postman). Replaces funnel thinking with gravitational metaphors.

**Love** (alignment + activity) × **Reach** (influence + connections) = **Gravity** (impact on growth)

Members pull themselves inward based on mission alignment, not push through conversion funnels. This reframe matters for developer communities where autonomy drives participation.

| Level | Name | Behavior Pattern | Typical % of Community |
|-------|------|-----------------|----------------------|
| **O4** | Observers | Lurking, minimal involvement, just joined or watching | 60-70% |
| **O3** | Participants | Occasional engagement — asks a question, attends an event, shares once | 20-25% |
| **O2** | Contributors | Regular participation — helps others, creates content, submits feedback | 8-12% |
| **O1** | Ambassadors | Core contributors with highest gravity — leaders, moderators, external advocates | 2-5% |

### Movement Triggers
- **O4→O3:** Personal welcome, direct question, invitation to a specific low-stakes activity, reducing the barrier for first response
- **O3→O2:** Recognition for a contribution, invitation to help, inclusion in a smaller group, recurring engagement opportunity
- **O2→O1:** Formal role (Champion/Ambassador), private access, direct team communication, co-creation opportunity
- **Downward movement:** Burnout, life changes, loss of mission alignment, feeling unappreciated, product friction

### Applying to Small Communities
For a community of N members, healthy approximate distribution:
- O1: 2-5% (need minimum 3-5 active O1s for community to function)
- O2: 8-12%
- O3: 20-25%
- O4: 60-70%

If you don't have enough O1s, that's always the #1 gap to close. Volunteer Champions are the highest-leverage community investment.

## 4. Community Participation Framework

Source: Jono Bacon, "People Powered."

Three interconnected sections, applied to **each persona individually**:

### A. The On-Ramp (Onboarding)
Six stages from stranger to first value delivery:

1. **Why Participate?** — Sell the community's value. What's in it for them? Website, social, events must answer this clearly.
2. **Setup Tools** — Set up necessary tools to start contributing (join platform, create account, navigate structure).
3. **Find an Opportunity** — Identify a meaningful first activity (respond to a prompt, share a project, ask a question).
4. **First Contribution** — Ship something valuable to both the individual and the community.
5. **Get Feedback** — Receive positive reinforcement (reply, reaction, feature, thank-you).
6. **Build Confidence** — Positive first experience creates momentum for repeat participation.

**The "Ten-Minute Rule":** How quickly can someone go from joining to delivering something of value? Minimize the distance from Step 1 to Step 4. If a new member can't reach meaningful participation within 10 minutes, the on-ramp has too much friction.

### B. Engagement Progression
Three states members move through:

- **Casual** — Low-frequency, low-commitment. Drops in when prompted. May leave easily.
- **Regular** — Consistent pattern. Returns weekly+. Has a routine with the community.
- **Core** — Deep investment. Identifies with the community. Would feel loss if it disappeared.

**Transition support:**
- Casual→Regular: Habit formation through recurring events, rituals, predictable content cadence, gentle nudge campaigns
- Regular→Core: Identity and ownership through leadership roles, co-creation, recognition, governance participation

### C. Incentivization
Layered rewards sustaining progression:

- **Intrinsic:** Learning, belonging, identity, mastery, purpose, status among peers
- **Extrinsic:** Credits, swag, early access, titles, beta features, direct team access
- **Social:** Reputation, influence, platform (speaking opportunities, features, spotlights)

Match incentives to persona motivation. A business owner cares about "help my business succeed." A developer cares about "make me more effective." A career-builder cares about "make me more hireable."

## 5. CMX 7P's Framework

Source: CMX (Community Industry). Comprehensive community planning and audit structure.

| P | Core Question | Design Considerations |
|---|--------------|----------------------|
| **Purpose** | Why does this community exist — for both organization and members? | Must be rooted in member needs, not just company goals. Format: "Our community exists to help [persona] achieve [outcome] by providing [value mechanism]." |
| **People** | Who are we gathering? | Demographics, motivations, workflows, personas. Developer personas differ from customer support personas differ from end-user personas. |
| **Place** | Where does gathering happen? | Async forums, real-time chat, events, hybrid. Platform choice signals culture. Discord signals energy/informality; Discourse signals depth/searchability; GitHub signals technical rigor. |
| **Participation** | What do members actually do? | Contribution ladders from lurking to leadership. Recurring activities, rituals, cadences. What does a "successful member" look like at each Orbit level? |
| **Policy** | What are the rules? | Values, code of conduct, moderation approach, conflict resolution. CoC importance increases with scale. Invest before you need it. |
| **Promotion** | How do people find us? | Discovery channels, onboarding flows, referral mechanics. Developer communities grow through docs and GitHub more than advertising. |
| **Performance** | How do we measure success? | Three-level measurement: business outcomes → community health → tactical metrics. See `references/metrics-and-health.md`. |

Use the 7P's for **quarterly strategic reviews** — audit each P against current state and identify gaps.

## 6. Community Roundtable Maturity Model

Source: The Community Roundtable. Assesses organizational readiness across eight competencies through four stages.

| Competency | Stage 1: Hierarchy | Stage 2: Emergent | Stage 3: Community | Stage 4: Networked |
|------------|-------------------|-------------------|--------------------|--------------------|
| **Strategy** | No formal strategy | Informal, reactive | Documented, funded | Integrated into business strategy |
| **Leadership** | No community leadership | Single champion | Dedicated team | Cross-functional ownership |
| **Culture** | Top-down only | Experimenting with openness | Community-informed decisions | Community co-creation |
| **Community Mgmt** | No dedicated role | Part-time / volunteer | Dedicated professional(s) | Scaled through volunteer leaders |
| **Content** | Company-generated only | Some member content | 60-80% member-generated | Community-driven content engine |
| **Policies** | None | Informal norms | Documented CoC + guidelines | Community-governed policies |
| **Tools** | Basic / ad hoc | Integrated platform(s) | Purpose-built stack | Automated and scaled |
| **Metrics** | None or vanity only | Basic tracking | Health metrics + business impact | Predictive analytics |

**Key rule:** Don't implement tactics above your maturity stage. A Stage 1 organization trying to run Stage 3 programs will fail and burn trust. Advance one stage at a time across all competencies.

## 7. Onboarding Journey Design

Source: Synthesized from Bacon, CMX, and DevRel best practices.

The most critical retention lever. Every community member's first 90 days determines whether they become a Regular or churn.

| Timeframe | Actions | Success Signal |
|-----------|---------|----------------|
| **First 24 hours** | Automated welcome, invitation to introduce themselves, clear value proposition, immediate small win (react, vote, answer a poll) | Member completes first action |
| **First week** | Personal outreach from community manager or Champion, invitation to upcoming event, introduction to relevant channels/resources | Member posts or asks a question |
| **First month** | Opportunity to contribute (answer a question, share work), invitation to event, feedback collection on their experience | Member creates a second touchpoint |
| **First 90 days** | Pathway to deeper involvement, recognition for contributions, connection to peer group, recruitment conversation if showing O2 behavior | Member becomes a Regular (consistent return pattern) |

**Developer-specific adaptations:**
- README/quickstart that delivers first success in under 5 minutes
- "Good First Issue" tags or easy challenges for new contributors
- CONTRIBUTING.md with clear setup instructions
- Labeled channels/categories so people can self-sort to their interest

## 8. Content Purpose Framework

Source: Synthesized from CMX, DevRel literature.

Categorize every piece of content. A healthy community maintains balance. If >80% is staff-generated Educational or Operational content, you have a content operation, not a community.

| Purpose | Definition | What It Does | Examples |
|---------|-----------|--------------|----------|
| **Educational** | Teaches something useful | Drives discovery and activation | Tutorials, guides, docs, livestream episodes, templates |
| **Conversational** | Sparks dialogue and relationships | Builds belonging and engagement | Discussion prompts, AMAs, polls, "what are you working on?" threads |
| **Showcasing** | Recognizes and features members/work | Provides recognition and inspires | Spotlights, competition winners, galleries, success stories |
| **Operational** | Maintains trust and transparency | Sustains trust and reduces uncertainty | Announcements, release notes, status updates, roadmaps, CoC |

**Health target:** 60-80% of community content should be member-generated. If your team creates most content, you haven't yet built a community — you've built a broadcast channel.

## 9. Community Persona Archetypes

Source: Synthesized from Bacon, CMX, FeverBee, open source community research.

Adapt these archetypes to your specific product and audience. Each persona needs its own on-ramp.

### Developer Community Personas
- **The Career Builder** (junior) — Motivated by learning and portfolio. Wants clear docs and "Good First Issue" tags. Contributes documentation fixes, attends networking events.
- **The Power User** — Motivated by solving personal problems and improving tools. Wants fast issue response and roadmap influence. Submits bug reports, PRs for needed features.
- **The Ideological Contributor** — Motivated by altruism and building the commons. Wants open governance and transparency. Provides sustained contributions, mentors newcomers.
- **The Corporate Contributor** — Paid by employer to contribute. Wants clear CLA/DCO, professional communication. Works business hours on employer-relevant features.

### Product Community Personas
- **The Business Builder** — Motivated by business outcomes. Wants templates, clear instructions, visible results.
- **The Explorer** — Motivated by curiosity and building cool things. Wants creative freedom and community recognition.
- **The Evaluator** — Motivated by finding tools for professional use. Wants documentation, reliability, comparisons.
- **The Reluctant User** — Motivated by necessity, intimidated by technology. Wants hand-holding, video walkthroughs, social proof.

## 10. Champion & Ambassador Program Design

Source: Competitive research (Lovable, Replit models), CMX ambassador design.

### Tiered Structure
| Tier | Name | Responsibilities | Benefits | Commitment |
|------|------|-----------------|----------|------------|
| **Tier 1** | Champions (Online) | Moderate community, welcome newcomers, answer questions | Private channel, beta access, credits, swag, title, direct team access | 3-5 hours/week |
| **Tier 2** | Ambassadors (IRL) | Host meetups, represent at conferences, run workshops | Travel stipend, speaker coaching, revenue share or referral program | 1-2 events/month |
| **Tier 3** | Experts Network | Client implementation, consulting, professional services | Lead generation, certification, premium support | Professional services |

### Recruitment Criteria
- Engagement frequency (active for 4+ weeks minimum)
- Content quality (helpful, constructive, accurate)
- Helpfulness to others (answering questions, welcoming newcomers)
- Mission alignment (believes in the product/community)
- Reach/influence (optional but valuable for Ambassadors)

### Burnout Prevention
- Spread recognition widely — don't over-rely on the same power users
- Recognize quality of contributions, not just quantity
- Set explicit time expectations upfront
- Provide easy off-ramps without permanent status loss
- Make recognition visible but not competitive
- Regular check-ins on workload and satisfaction

### GTM for Ambassador Programs
- **Recruitment sources:** CRM mining for high-engagement users, social listening, NPS promoters, support power users, existing member referrals
- **Selection:** Application → screening → interview → trial period → acceptance
- **Cohort vs rolling:** Cohorts provide synchronized community and milestones; rolling provides flexibility

## 11. Cold Start Strategy

Source: Synthesized from Bacon ("People Powered"), CMX, DevRel practice.

The "empty disco" problem: people are uncomfortable being first to engage. At sub-500 members, this is the primary growth constraint.

1. **Recruit 10-15 founding members** committed to daily participation before promoting for growth. These become your first O1 Ambassadors.
2. **Seed content before opening doors** — 2+ weeks of active discussion history before inviting broadly. Nobody wants to walk into a silent room.
3. **Model engagement for 18+ months minimum** — The community manager must be the most active poster, questioner, and responder until organic participation exceeds staff participation.
4. **Direct outreach over broadcast** — Personal messages to active product users ("I saw you built X — impressive. Would you share how you did it?") outperform mass announcements 10:1.
5. **Concentrate energy** — A 20-person channel that's buzzing feels alive. A 500-person server with 3 messages/day feels dead. Consider focused channels, cohort onboarding, or smaller "founding member" groups to concentrate energy before scaling.
6. **Convert existing product users** — The warmest audience is people already using the product. Map your active user base and create a pathway from product to community.

## 12. DevRel-Sales Alignment

Source: Lewko & Parton, "How to Build and Grow a Successful Developer Program."

Three frameworks work together for Go-To-Market:

1. **Developer Journey stages** — The developer's perspective (Discover→Scale). Their needs come first.
2. **DevRel objectives** — The DevRel team's mission (Awareness→Retention).
3. **Sales funnel** — The business perspective (Lead→Close→Expand).

These three map onto each other:
- **Discover + Awareness** → Top of funnel (Lead generation)
- **Evaluate + Activation** → Mid funnel (Qualification, trial)
- **Learn/Build + Engagement** → Pipeline (Active evaluation, POC)
- **Scale + Retention** → Post-sale (Expansion, advocacy)

**Key principle:** DevRel builds trust and awareness at the top; sales converts at the bottom. DevRel should never be asked to "close deals" and sales should never be asked to "build community." But they must share data, language, and goals. DevRel-generated leads (Developer Relations Qualified Leads / DRQLs) should flow to sales with context about the developer's journey stage, use case, and engagement history.
